# Peer Review — *Time and Chance: The Elements of Scale in the Green Transition*

Manuscript of 27 chapters, five parts, 38,887 words. Reviewed for quality
of read and for gaps in the argument. References are to chapter numbers
in the current manuscript.

---

## Summary Judgment

This is a book with one strong idea and the discipline, mostly, to
follow it. The idea is that the cost of capital, not technology or the
quantity of money, is the binding constraint on the transition in the
countries that matter; that the cost of capital is set by risk; and that
a large part of that risk is either informational or institutional and
can therefore be engineered away. The preface states this cleanly, and
the best chapters — 2, 4, 7, 9, 16 and 24 — earn it.

The manuscript has two problems that a reader will feel before they can
name them. The first is repetition: the same handful of insights are
re-presented in successive chapters under different headings, because
those chapters began life as separate memos. The second is that the
book's central promise — that risk can be absorbed "without using up"
anyone's balance sheet — is asserted far more often than it is
demonstrated, and the manuscript's own best chapter on risk (3) contains
the objection that the later chapters never answer.

Both are fixable. The first is an editing problem. The second is an
argument problem, and it is the one that a hostile reviewer would lead
with.

---

## What Works

**The thesis is stated and sustained.** Few policy books can be reduced
to one sentence without loss. This one can, and the preface does it. The
70-versus-10 percentage point debt figure is well chosen as the number
to hang the argument on.

**The bond-like observation.** Chapter 2's point — that a completed,
contracted, operating solar park has the cash flow profile of a bond,
and that the gap between what it actually pays and what that profile
deserves is the value the whole book exists to capture — is the
manuscript's best single sentence. It should be on the cover copy.

**Contracts beat prices.** Chapter 4's reframing, that a reverse auction
is not a second-best substitute for a carbon price but does something a
price structurally cannot do (create a contract, and therefore a
financeable asset), is genuinely useful and not widely made. It also
justifies the book's title claim about timing: quantity instruments
coordinate across time.

**Guarantee the offtaker, not the loan.** Chapter 9's argument for why
the guarantee should sit at the power purchase agreement rather than at
the debt — efficiency, preserved recourse, incentive alignment — is
precise and correct, and the SECI case gives it a working example.

**The credit-ledger principle.** The idea that a default to a repeat
lender becomes a ledger entry rather than a credit event (Chapters 7,
10, 16) is the mechanism that makes the concessionality claim plausible,
and it is explained well.

**Funding versus financing.** Chapter 15's distinction, and its
observation that solar's cost collapse has moved a large class of
projects from the funding category to the financing category, is the
cleanest statement anywhere in the book of why the derisking agenda now
does work that used to require grants.

**The theory of concessionality.** Chapter 16's claim that
concessionality can be manufactured out of credit ratings,
relationships and diversification rather than paid for from aid budgets
is the book's most original contribution and deserves more space than
the 900 words it gets.

**Chapter 24.** The argument that a fuel import commitment is an unbooked
liability, and that borrowing to build solar converts a variable,
foreign-currency, open-ended obligation into a fixed, finite, amortizing
one, is exactly right and is the sharpest rebuttal available to the
debt-sustainability objection. It should be brought forward.

## Quality of Read

### The repetition problem

The manuscript proposes what is essentially one equity buffer fund at
least five times, under five names: the Global Climate Risk Equity Fund
(Chapter 11), the clean energy equity fund (12), the global equity fund
for policy lending (20), the Climate Investment and Insurance Fund (21),
and the Climate Incentives Trust (22). The variations between them are
real — the CIT adds international incentives and the trust structure;
the CIIF is a Financial Intermediary Fund; Chapter 20's fund absorbs
policy-lending risk specifically — but a reader encountering the fifth
version has stopped being able to tell whether they are reading a new
proposal or a restatement. The same is true of the "guarantee the
offtaker" insight, which appears in Chapters 7, 9, 10, 15 and 16, and of
the "used but not used up" phrase for the World Bank's credit rating.

The fix is not to cut the chapters — each contains material the others
lack — but to make the relationship explicit. A short passage at the
head of Part IV saying "Parts III and IV describe one structure, seen
from four angles; here is how the pieces map onto each other" would
convert the repetition from a defect into a design. A single diagram of
the full risk stack, appearing once and referred to thereafter, would do
more for the reader than any amount of prose.

Chapter 18, "Five Mechanisms," compounds this. It summarizes Chapters
20–23 before they arrive. Either it should be moved to the end of Part
IV as a consolidation, or it should be folded into the Part IV opening.

### Register

Parts I–III are written for a general policy reader. Part IV shifts,
sometimes mid-chapter, to a reader who already knows what DPF, PforR,
FIF and the Evolution Roadmap are. Chapter 19 in particular reads as a
briefing for Bank management — "we search for needed institutions where
there is a gap" — and sits oddly as a standalone chapter. The institutional
detail is valuable and should stay, but the acronyms need glossing on
first use and the Bank-internal framing needs to be translated into the
book's own voice.

### Thin chapters

Chapter 17 (FAQ) is 300 words and answers four questions the surrounding
chapters already answer. Chapter 8 (coal and PPAs) is 400 words and
would sit better as a section of Chapter 27. Chapter 14 (Green Policy as
Growth Policy) is the book's macroeconomic thesis — the green safe asset
— and at 500 words it is underweight for what it claims; it also sits
after the Sollar, which is its most speculative application, rather
than before it.

### The ending

The book ends on Chapter 27's list of "what still needs work" — carbon
pricing, coordination, financial assumptions, subsidy persistence,
success stories. This is honest but it is not an ending. The manuscript
has no conclusion. It needs one, and the material for it exists: the
preface's "Time and Chance" passage on thresholds and dynamics, and the
argument (currently in the working papers, in the green growth evidence
chapter) that the measured growth benefit of renewable deployment
appears only above a threshold of investment. A closing chapter that
returned to the 4-percent-versus-12-percent worlds, with the threshold
evidence, would close the loop the preface opens.

## Gaps in the Argument

Ordered by how much they threaten the thesis.

### 1. Where does the risk go?

This is the central gap. The manuscript's own Chapter 3 states the
objection precisely: "many transfer strategies work normally almost all
of the time and then fail together. They have tail risks: the
counterparty that was diversified turns out to have been correlated." It
then observes that "a structure that relies entirely on transfer with no
reduction is a structure that has hidden its risk rather than dealt
with it."

The later chapters do not answer this about their own structures. The
three-layer stack — sovereign first loss, equity fund second loss, AAA
guarantor third — is presented as consuming no one's balance sheet.
But risk is conserved. Diversification across thirty jurisdictions
reduces idiosyncratic risk; it does nothing for the systemic case in
which a global rate shock, a dollar spike, or an emerging market
currency crisis hits every PPA at once. That is precisely the case in
which the equity buffer is exhausted and the AAA guarantor pays. The
book needs a chapter, or at least a section of Chapter 16, that walks
through the correlated-shock scenario honestly: what breaks, who pays,
and how large the exposure is. Chapter 11's own assessment criteria
demand it — "it remains crucial to know who ultimately bears the risk"
— and then nothing does.

### 2. The fiscal-space claim contradicts the first-loss design

Chapter 11 states that the structure "does not increase developing
country government liabilities." Chapter 21 makes the developing country
government the first-loss guarantor, indemnifying the World Bank.
Chapter 9's sovereign indemnity does the same. These are contingent
liabilities, and contingent liabilities are what debt sustainability
frameworks — the subject of Chapter 25 — are increasingly designed to
capture.

The book's real position, which is defensible, is that these
liabilities are *conditional on the government's own policy choices*
and therefore are not risks in the ordinary sense — a government
guaranteeing only what it controls. But that argument is made only in
passing, and the flat "no increase in liabilities" claim should be
retired. The manuscript is stronger admitting the contingent liability
and arguing that it is the right kind.

### 3. Currency risk is named as unsolvable and then set aside

Chapter 9 calls currency mismatch "the risk that cannot be guaranteed
away" and offers two mitigants: the declining PPA and local-currency
denomination for the domestic savings market. Chapter 21 says the CIIF
would be "structured to absorb foreign exchange risk" without saying
how. Chapter 13 offers the Sollar as the radical answer but flags it as
speculative. Chapter 14 proposes a green numéraire in five hundred
words.

The hardest problem in the book receives its thinnest treatment. A
serious version needs at least: an estimate of what fraction of the
emerging market cost-of-capital premium is currency risk as against
credit risk; a discussion of the existing tools (TCX, local currency
guarantee facilities, partial indexation); and an honest statement of
whether the book's structures reduce currency risk or merely reallocate
it to the guarantor.

### 4. The load-bearing numbers are asserted, not derived

The preface says a solar park costs roughly twice as much per kWh at 12
percent as at 4 percent. This is broadly right, but nowhere in the book
is a levelized cost of electricity actually computed as a function of
the discount rate. One table — LCOE for a representative project at 4,
8 and 12 percent — would do more to convince a sceptical reader than
the dozen places where the claim is restated. Similarly, the LSE
70-versus-10 figure carries the preface and is cited from a single
study without any discussion of its assumptions.

Chapter 20's worked example (return on equity doubling from a 4 percent
refinancing) and Chapter 22's financial summary (the trust doubling
every two years; $4 trillion of leveraged investment) are flagged as
"tentative and indicative," which is appropriate. But the book's
credibility rests on the reader believing that the mechanism is
realistic even if the numbers are illustrative, and the mechanism is
never stress-tested. What happens to the doubling if refinancing is
available at 6 percent rather than 4? At 8?

### 5. Interest rates have moved

The manuscript was drafted across a period that began in a low-rate
environment — Chapter 11 still refers to "a low-interest environment
currently anyways" — and the refinancing arithmetic everywhere assumes
that AAA-rated paper can be placed at around 4 percent. In 2026 that
assumption is a live question rather than a given. The argument
survives higher rates — the *gap* between a developing country's cost of
capital and the AAA rate is what matters, not the level — but the book
should say so explicitly and rerun its central example at current
rates.

### 6. The evidence base is one case

The book argues almost entirely from mechanism. The only worked
institutional example is SECI, and the book asserts rather than shows
that the model would port to Nigeria or Brazil, which lack India's
federal structure, central bank credit lines and state-government
indemnity chain. Chapter 10, titled "Key Insights from Existing
Structures," cites no existing structure other than SECI.

There is a substantial empirical record the book does not use. South
Africa's REIPPPP, Brazil's and Chile's auction programmes, Mexico's
pre-2018 auctions, Scaling Solar in Zambia and Senegal — these are
exactly the natural experiments the thesis predicts should show falling
cost of capital as track record accumulates. If the spreads compressed,
the book's "chance" argument (that much of the premium is ignorance
rather than hazard) is confirmed. If they did not, the argument needs
revising. Either way, the book should look. And SECI's own record on
Discom arrears deserves an honest paragraph: the model has *moved*
payment risk, which the book argues is the point, but it has not
eliminated it.

### 7. The "chance" claim is not decomposed

The preface's most striking claim is that a large part of the developing
country risk premium is "the price of an absent track record — of
lenders having no basis on which to judge, and therefore charging for
their ignorance." This is the claim that makes the book's title earn its
place. It is also an empirical claim about the decomposition of a risk
premium into a real component and an informational one, and the book
never attempts the decomposition. Point 6 above is where the evidence
would come from.

### 8. The declining PPA shifts risk without saying to whom

The declining-tariff design (Chapters 9, 11, 24) is presented as the
solution to PPA fiscal risk. It is, for the offtaker. But a contract
whose price falls on a schedule set in advance is a bet that solar
costs will fall at least as fast as the schedule. If they do not —
supply chain disruption, mineral constraints, which Chapter 9 itself
mentions — the developer is squeezed. Who bears that risk, and does it
raise the cost of capital by enough to offset the benefit? The book
should say.

### 9. Grid, curtailment and storage

Chapter 16 admits that grids and batteries "remain to be done." That is
fair for a first book. But a PPA in a weak grid faces curtailment risk —
the plant is contracted but the grid cannot take the power — and this is
a *revenue* risk that sits inside the contracted stream the whole book
relies on. At minimum, the book should note that "take-or-pay" versus
"take-and-pay" contract terms determine whether curtailment risk falls
on the offtaker or the developer, and that the bond-like quality of the
cash flow depends on the answer.

### 10. Distribution of the gains

The classic critique of derisking — socialized risk, privatized return —
is answered in Chapter 16 by the public equity fund's participation. But
in Chapter 11's own worked example the fund holds an option on $150,000
of a $1 million project, and Chapter 11 also says initial high
profitability for private investors is desirable to recruit capital.
Both claims are reasonable. Together they invite the question of what
share of the cost-of-capital saving actually reaches consumers or the
public fund, and over what horizon. A paragraph quantifying the split in
the worked example would pre-empt the objection.

## Recommendations, in Priority Order

1. **Write the risk chapter.** Answer Chapter 3's tail-risk objection
   about the book's own structures: the correlated-shock case, who pays,
   how much. This is the difference between a proposal and an argument.

2. **Write the conclusion.** Return to the two worlds — 4 percent and
   12 percent — and close with the threshold evidence. The book
   currently stops rather than ends.

3. **Add one LCOE-versus-discount-rate table** and one risk-stack
   diagram. Refer to both throughout instead of restating.

4. **Add an evidence chapter** on auction programmes with a track
   record, or fold the evidence into Chapter 10 and make its title
   honest.

5. **Consolidate the equity fund.** Open Part IV with a map of how
   Chapters 11, 12, 20, 21 and 22 relate. Move or absorb Chapter 18.

6. **Retire the "no increase in liabilities" claim** and replace it with
   the conditional-liability argument, made explicitly.

7. **Expand currency risk** into a proper section, engaging existing
   tools and stating plainly what the book's structures do and do not
   do about it.

8. **Rerun the central arithmetic at 2026 rates** and say why the
   argument survives.

9. **Gloss the Bank acronyms** on first use and translate Chapter 19
   out of memo register.

10. **Merge Chapter 8 into Chapter 27**, expand Chapter 14 or move it to
    open Part III, and either expand Chapter 17 or cut it.

## Minor

- Chapter 15 shares its title with the book's subtitle. Consider
  renaming the chapter.
- Chapter 15 still carries "*Note: the remainder of this chapter is work
  in progress.*" Remove before circulation.
- Chapter 22's "post-2050 populations" as beneficiaries raises
  governance questions — who represents them, who controls the accounts
  in the interim, what prevents capture — that a reader will ask and the
  chapter does not answer.
- Chapter 25's ten recommendations are a list from the literature, not
  the book's own; either mark them as such more clearly or cut to the
  ones the book actually argues for.
- Adaptation is absent throughout. That is a legitimate scope choice
  but should be stated once, in the preface.
- The book cites Wright's law, Olson, Schumpeter and Ramsey by name but
  has no bibliography of its own; the consolidated bibliography is in
  the working papers. It needs a short references section.
